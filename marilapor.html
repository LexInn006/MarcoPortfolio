MariLapor! · Mobile App

Making everyday reporting feel like it actually goes somewhere

A civic reporting app that turns flagging a pothole or a broken streetlight into something open, trackable, and worth doing — instead of a form that disappears into a black box.

The problem

Reporting felt like shouting into a void

Existing ways of reporting public issues tend to follow the same pattern: a report goes in, and then it disappears into a closed process only the reporter and the handler can see. There's no real incentive to keep flagging small, low-impact issues, and problems usually only get attention once they've gone viral on social media rather than through the report itself.

The solution

A reporting feed people actually want to use

MariLapor! opens the process up. Reports live in a public, forum-style feed that anyone can browse, upvote, and share — closer to a community feed than a government form. Verified reports earn points, redeemable for real vouchers, giving people an actual reason to keep reporting, even when the issue is small.

Background

Where the idea came from

MariLapor! started as a Software Engineering course project for a team of five, built following standard SDLC principles. The idea itself came out of a team brainstorm rather than a single personal incident — the team looked at how public issue-reporting typically works and kept landing on the same gaps: poor navigation for non-technical users, a closed reporting process, no incentive to report minor issues, and a reliance on social media virality to get anything fixed.

That gap between quietly filing a report and actually seeing something happen became the starting point for the product.

Research

Kept deliberately lightweight

With four weeks from concept to a working app, research stayed lean by design. Instead of running formal interviews or a competitor teardown, the team relied on a structured brainstorm to map out what makes public reporting feel discouraging, then translated those pain points directly into product decisions — the open feed, the upvotes, and the points system all trace back to this stage.

Define

Designing around one clear user

Dimas Pratama, 20
University student, rides a motorbike around campus daily
  • Regularly notices small issues on the road — a dangling cable, a pothole, a broken streetlight
  • Rarely reports them, because reporting usually feels like a black box — submit and forget
  • Wants reporting to feel more like social media: a community, visible progress, and some form of appreciation
How might we make reporting public issues feel transparent and rewarding, so people keep reporting even when the issue is small?
Ideation

Staying disciplined about scope

Rather than brainstorming a long feature list and cutting it down, the team stayed disciplined from the start and only built what the core experience actually needed: account creation, report submission with photo and location, an open community feed, upvotes, and a leaderboard-and-points system to keep people engaged. Marco specifically proposed the leaderboard and points system — rewarding verified, resolved reports with points redeemable for e-vouchers or merchandise, designed to sustain long-term reporting behavior rather than one-off use.

Design

From rough concept to a polished, modern UI

The black-and-orange system was a deliberate choice for a modern, simple feel rather than something more formal or municipal. Three teammates built the initial concept screens; Marco's role was taking that rough draft and refining it into the final, polished UI shown throughout this page.

Testing & iteration

Refinement happened before testing, not after

Because the design had already gone through the refinement pass shown above, the version tested by friends and the course lecturer was close to final. Both said it worked well, and no further revisions were flagged at that stage — the team didn't collect formal metrics or testimonials from this round.

Final thoughts

Looking back

Learned

How to design a UI that feels simple and modern to a contemporary audience — and how to think beyond the interface, toward systems (like points and leaderboards) that give people a reason to keep coming back.

Next steps

Launching on the Play Store as a real, everyday tool — ideally integrated with local government workflows so verified reports can reach the right office directly.

Proudest of

The final UI — clean and comfortable to look at — and how solid the team's process felt, from ideation all the way to a fully working app.
