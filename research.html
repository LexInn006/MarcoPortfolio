Research · IEEE Paper

Bad design doesn't just look worse — people stop trusting it

A comparative study testing whether a website's interface quality changes how much students trust the accuracy of the information on it. Short answer: it does, by a wide margin.

Year
2026
Duration
12 Weeks
Role
Main Author, Researcher & UI Prototype Designer
View Open Data
The problem

Trust is often assumed, rarely tested

Plenty of advice exists on what makes a "good" website UI, but little of it directly tests whether that quality actually changes how much people trust the information presented — especially in the context of news and information media, where misplaced trust has real consequences.

The approach

Build two versions, ask real people

Rather than surveying opinions about UI in the abstract, the study built two working prototypes of the same news website — one deliberately well-designed, one deliberately poor — and measured how differently 116 students rated their trust in each.

Background & motivation

Why this topic, why students

The topic came from a shared interest: both Marco and his co-author, Ignatius Roger Ramopolii, are Computer Science/Multimedia students drawn to UI. A literature search turned up plenty of general UI-quality research, but nothing that specifically tested UI quality against trust in the context of information or news media — that gap became the paper's starting point.

The study deliberately focused on students rather than the general public. As students themselves, the researchers felt they understood that population's context well enough to design around it — a general-public sample would have introduced far more variables than a 12-week project could account for.

The paper was supervised by Irma Kartika Wairooy and Brilly Andro Makalew, submitted to a conference, and was not accepted. Marco doesn't plan to resubmit it — he treats it as a research and data-analysis learning experience rather than a publication to pursue further, though he expects the UI/UX knowledge from the literature review has likely shaped his design instincts on other projects since, even subconsciously.

Methodology

Two prototypes, one questionnaire

Marco and his co-author co-designed and built two full website prototypes — a professional, well-structured "Good UI" version and a deliberately poor "Bad UI" version — then ran a Likert-scale questionnaire asking Greater Jakarta students to rate each on visual aesthetics, layout, consistency, navigation, overall UI quality, and digital trust.

The hardest part of building the prototypes wasn't the Good UI — it was making the Bad UI look naturally bad, without tipping into obvious exaggeration. As Multimedia students trained to design well, deliberately designing badly took real restraint.

Marco personally built and ran the Google Forms questionnaire and handled the statistical analysis himself, using Welch's t-test to compare the two groups.

Data collection

A late scramble for responses

Collection didn't go smoothly. With three days left before the deadline, the study had only around 50 of its 100+ target responses. Rather than wait it out, Marco took the initiative to distribute the questionnaire through a friend at a different university, rather than relying solely on his own campus network — a decision that helped push the final count to 116 respondents.

Results

The gap was large, and statistically significant

Across every measured dimension, the Good UI prototype scored dramatically higher than the Bad UI version — and the two headline metrics held up under Welch's t-test.

Overall UI Quality (mean)
1.92→4.35
Bad UI → Good UI · t(Welch) = 28.75, p < 0.001
Digital Trust Level (mean)
1.97→4.42
Bad UI → Good UI · t(Welch) = 23.95, p < 0.001

Final thoughts

Looking back

Learned

End-to-end research experience — framing a question the literature hadn't answered yet, designing an experiment to test it, collecting real data under a tight deadline, and running the statistics to back it up.

Outcome

Submitted to a conference and not accepted. Marco isn't pursuing resubmission — the value was in the process itself, and in how the underlying UI/UX research likely still shapes his design decisions on other projects.

Hardest part

Designing the "Bad UI" prototype convincingly — deliberately building something that looks naturally, not exaggeratedly, poorly designed.
